Vector-Valued Functions — Position, Velocity & Acceleration

Differentiate a moving point's position vector frame-by-frame to uncover its velocity and acceleration as vectors in their own right.

Intuition

Picture an animation: each frame updates a dot's position a little. The position itself is a vector, $\vec{r}(t)$, pointing from the origin to the dot. But how that vector changes from frame to frame — its own rate of change — is just as meaningful. That rate of change is a vector too, and it's called velocity.

Core concept

Differentiating a vector-valued function works component by component: differentiate $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ separately, then bundle the results back into a vector. $\vec{v}(t) = \vec{r}\,'(t)$ gives the velocity vector — its direction is the direction of motion, and its length is the speed. Differentiate again and you get $\vec{a}(t) = \vec{r}\,''(t)$, the acceleration vector, describing how the velocity itself is changing.

Key Idea

$$ \vec{v}(t) = \vec{r}\,'(t) = \big\langle x'(t),\, y'(t) \big\rangle, \qquad \vec{a}(t) = \vec{r}\,''(t) = \big\langle x''(t),\, y''(t) \big\rangle $$

Both derivatives are still vectors, found by differentiating each component on its own. Velocity tracks how position changes frame to frame; acceleration tracks how velocity itself changes frame to frame — the same idea applied one level deeper.

$\vec{r}(t)$The position vector at parameter value $t$
$\vec{v}(t)$The velocity vector — direction and speed of motion
$\vec{a}(t)$The acceleration vector — how the velocity vector is changing
$|\vec{v}(t)|$Speed — the length of the velocity vector, with direction stripped away

Think Further

  1. At the peak of the projectile's path (t = 2), predict what the vertical component of the velocity vector equals. Why does that make sense geometrically?
  2. A designer animating an object along a Bezier path cares about $\vec{r}\,'(t)$, not just $\vec{r}(t)$. What would knowing only the position, and not the velocity vector, fail to tell them about the animation?
  3. The acceleration vector here stays constant at ⟨0, −10⟩ for the whole flight. Predict what that implies about how the velocity vector's direction changes over time, versus its vertical component's rate of change.
Show suggested answers
  1. It equals zero. At the peak, the dot is momentarily moving purely horizontally — neither rising nor falling — which is exactly the geometric meaning of a flat tangent at the top of the arc.
  2. Position alone says where the object is, but not how fast or in what direction it's moving through that point — information the velocity vector supplies, which controls how smooth or abrupt the animation feels frame to frame.
  3. Since acceleration is constant, the vertical component of velocity changes at a steady rate throughout the flight, while the horizontal component never changes at all — so the velocity vector's direction steadily rotates from angled-upward, through horizontal at the peak, to angled-downward.
