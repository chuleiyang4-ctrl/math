Module B1.4

Variables & Multiplication as Scaling

A letter in a formula isn't hiding a secret — it's just an empty box waiting for a number. And multiplying that number by something isn't repeated adding — it's turning a dial that stretches or shrinks it.

1

Intuition First

A recipe card says "2 cups flour, serves 4." Those numbers aren't fixed forever — they're really "per one batch" amounts, sitting in an invisible box labeled flour needed. Whatever number you're feeding, the box holds however much flour that requires. That box is exactly what a variable is: not a mystery, just a labeled slot waiting for whatever number applies to your situation. When mathematicians write x, they mean the same thing as that recipe card's blank — "put the relevant number here."

Now say you want to serve 10 people instead of 4. You don't re-derive the recipe from scratch — you find a scale factor (10 ÷ 4 = 2.5) and multiply every ingredient by it. This is the real meaning of multiplication: not just "adding a number to itself repeatedly," but stretching or shrinking a quantity by some factor. Multiply by 2.5 and everything gets 2.5 times bigger. Multiply by 0.5 and everything shrinks to half. On the number line from B1.1, multiplying is what happens when you grab a point and drag it farther from (or closer to) zero — and multiplying by a negative number, like in B1.2, flips it to the other side entirely.

2

A Recipe You Can Scale by Hand

The recipe needs 2 cups of flour for 4 servings. You're cooking for 10 people. How much flour do you need?

scale factor = 10 ÷ 4= 2.5
new flour = 2 × 2.5= 5 cups
every ingredient×2.5

Every ingredient in the recipe gets multiplied by the same scale factor — that's what keeps the dish tasting the same, just bigger. If flour were the variable x = 2 and the scale factor were called c = 2.5, the new amount is simply c·x.

Formal Definition

A variable is a symbol standing in for a number that isn't fixed — it can be filled in with whatever value the situation calls for:

Multiplying a variable by a fixed number c — a scalar — stretches or shrinks it by that factor. If c is negative, the result also flips to the other side of zero:

xa variable — an empty box waiting for a specific number
ca scalar — the fixed number doing the stretching or shrinking
c·xx scaled by c — bigger if |c|>1, smaller if |c|<1, flipped if c is negative
c=1the "do-nothing" scale — leaves x exactly as it was
